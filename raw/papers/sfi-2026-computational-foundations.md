# Computational foundations of the human world

**Authors:** Multiple authors from Santa Fe Institute, et al.

**Date:** May 2026

**Subjects:** Social and Information Networks (cs.SI); Systems and Control (eess.SY); Populations and Evolution (q-bio.PE)

**License:** CC BY 4.0

## Abstract

Human societies continuously transform scattered information into collective judgments and coordinated action, whether through markets discovering prices, governments allocating resources, communities enforcing norms, or science converging on reliable claims. Importantly, the computational difficulty of collective decision-making, particularly the time and communication required to reach solutions, imposes fundamental constraints on social organization. While theoretical computer science offers formal tools for analyzing such problems, there is surprisingly no domain of social science that focuses on the nature of computation in the human world. This perspective argues that we now have the opportunity to deploy these computational frameworks to study human social organization.

## Core Argument

Human societies are **distributed computational systems** that aggregate dispersed information, knowledge, and skills to solve problems of collective behavior. These problems broadly include:
- Resource allocation
- Collective decision-making
- Integration of specialized skills and occupations

## Four Computational Social Phenomena

1. **Distributed consensus and coordinated action** — how groups reach decisions without central authority
2. **Societal restructuring with scale** — as societies grow, they develop specialized roles and institutional infrastructures to increase computational power
3. **Hierarchical and modular structure** — organizational forms that manage computational complexity
4. **Externalized memory systems** — how societies store and retrieve collective knowledge (writing, institutions, databases)

## Key Intellectual Moves

1. Hayek: Markets as information-processing systems
2. Durkheim: Division of labor as computational specialization
3. Arrow: Social choice theory formalizes aggregation problems
4. SFI tradition: Scaling laws, allometric approaches, quantitative historical dynamics

## Distinction from Existing Fields
- NOT the same as computational social science (applying computation to study society)
- NOT the study of algorithms in society
- NOT the design of algorithms for social benefit
- IS: The computational aspects of social phenomena themselves — understanding society AS computation
